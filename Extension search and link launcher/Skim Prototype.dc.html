Reading at scroll speed

By the interface group  ·  9 minute read  ·  Subscribe

The gap between finding and reading

Browser find has not changed in fifteen years. It locates a string and parks it wherever the scroll happens to land, which is often a single line above the fold or hidden behind a sticky header. The result is a small burst of latency in the reader's head: the text was found, but the reading has not started yet.

That pause is cheap once and expensive forty times. A researcher moving through a long specification will jump between matches until the shape of the argument appears. Every jump that lands badly costs a re-orientation, and re-orientation is the slowest thing a reader does.

Measuring the jump

We instrumented three hundred find sessions and timed the interval between a match being highlighted and the reader's gaze settling on the surrounding sentence. Median latency was 740 milliseconds when the match landed in the upper third of the viewport, and 1.9 seconds when it landed within one line of an edge.

The fix is geometric rather than algorithmic. Reserve a band of the viewport that the overlay never covers, and offset the scroll so the match always arrives inside that band with its neighbours intact. Nothing about the matching changes; only the arithmetic of where the page stops.

Rule 01 · clear zone
The overlay owns the upper band. The match is placed in the middle of whatever is left.

What the eye needs

A match is not a destination, it is a coordinate. Readers want the line above it and the two lines below it, because that is the unit they use to decide whether to stop. Centring the match in the free band gives them all three without asking them to scroll again.

Matches inside a nested scroll region need the same treatment applied twice: the inner container scrolls first, then the page positions the container. Matches inside collapsed regions need the region opened before any arithmetic is attempted, because a closed region has no height to aim at.

When the page has already delivered the match into the clear band, the correct amount of scrolling is none. Moving the page under a reader who is already looking at the right place is the most annoying failure mode of all, and the easiest to avoid.

+ Appendix A — test harness

The harness replays recorded scroll traces against a headless renderer and reports the latency between highlight and settle for each strategy. Collapsed sections are opened programmatically before measurement, which is why the appendix is itself collapsed in this demo.

Notes

Numbers in this piece come from an internal sample and have not been reviewed externally. Traces are available on request.

The last line of a document is the hardest target on the page: there is no scroll left to spend, so the overlay itself has to move. That is the only case where the launcher gives up its position, and the latency budget is preserved by raising it to the top edge instead.
